Ulises in partnership with Studio 105 presents Portals, co-curated by Connie Yu and Maya Yu Zhang, of the collective [FORTUNE](https://printingfortunes.info/), on view until September 20th.

Portals features an intergenerational group of artists, connected by the material and metaphorical project of making a home in an unfamiliar place. In Portals, reverberations of immigration, landing, and the ways these experiences have shaped our behavior emerge: formal drawing and painting grow loose and uncanny. Experimental poetics return to a material place. Mark-making techniques like debossing and scratching demark time. The picture plane is a window there, a wall here, a long slice of floor. 

Yu Jiangang’s paintings on paper, carried with him during his 1989 immigration from Shanghai to San Francisco, waver between the draft and the abstract, gesturing toward yards, hills, walls in middle distance, and amalgamated spaces that seem familiar in their pastoral. His kin, [Connie Yu](https://connieyu.one/Home), shares ink rubbings and prints on rice paper, using floorboards, windows, breeze blocks — skins of lived environments, surfaces both interior and exterior. Maya Yu Zhang’s works of paper comprise recycled and reformed immigration documents, trawled through natural processes on this side of the earth. Applying pressure to binaries of life/death, self/other, near/far, Portals engages with the additional valences of surface, with drawings by [Mei Kazama](https://meikazama.com/2024), who investigates the spectral as a realm of simultaneous grief and possibility. 

Artist Bios

[Mei Kazama](https://meikazama.com/2024) (b. 1994, New York) is an artist from New York City. They investigate the spectral as a realm of simultaneous grief and possibility, with a focus on drawing as a critical medium and framework. They also work as an educator, and their teaching brings together art and critical thinking as a way to develop long term self efficacy. They are currently serving as faculty at Cleveland Institute of Art as an AICAD Fellow.

[Connie Yu](https://connieyu.one/Home) (b.1995, New York) is a writer and artist based in Philadelphia. Their practice takes place in poetry and printmaking, cooking and strength training, and takes shape from the administrative aspects of these data — asking the limits and series of work and its measure, measure and its work. A 2025 recipient of the Leeway Transformation Award, they have shown in group exhibitions at ArtYard, William Way LGBT Community Center, Asian Arts Initiative, TILT Institute for the Contemporary Image, AUTOMAT, and Vox Populi. Read their writing in Hole to Hole (Many Folds Press, 2026), the chapbook modest technologies, 1 thru 3 (Neighboring Systems, 2019), and in the online directory Conflicts of Interest. 

Yu Jiangang (b.1959, Shanghai, China) currently resides in Pennsylvania, USA. He graduated from the Art Department of Shanghai Normal University in 1982 and subsequently taught there. He emigrated to the United States in 1989. During the 1980s, Yu participated in numerous art exhibitions in Shanghai. In 1986, his oil painting Hot Ground was featured in the Shanghai Art Exhibition, and the work was selected for inclusion in the major catalog Chinese Oil Painting (1786–1986). In 1987, his work Hot Ground No. 2 was exhibited at the First National Oil Painting Exhibition. The six works on display here were created by Yu thirty-seven years ago, in 1989, a time when he was undergoing a major life transition; indeed, at the end of that same year, he moved to the United States, where he has lived ever since. These works embody his aspirations for a new world and his vision for a new life. This is his first exhibition in the United States. 

Maya Yu Zhang (b.1991, Zhengzhou, China) is an artist and filmmaker who works with cinema as a loom. Often propelled by a simple desire to follow that which moves, they make and weave images of bodies in vulnerability, ecstasy, madness, and abandon to compose an unapologetically queer cadence of seeing, being, and healing. Their moving images collapse physical and psychological landscapes to manifest the perennial haunting of matricide at the hands of techno-patriarchal capitalism and post-socialist authoritarianism. Employing ephemera, photographs, speech, performance, archival recordings, and documentary footage, Maya instigates an archeology of bodily remains by excavating the hidden historical dimensions in the embodied present. Their practice can also take the form of writing, installations, music, and collective rituals, which are expansions or developments of their films.
